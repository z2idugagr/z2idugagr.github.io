Simplified to Taiwan Traditional Chinese: A Human Review Service That Catches What Conversion Misses

Hank Chen ・ November 26, 2026 ・ About 6 minutes

You already have a Simplified Chinese version — of your website, your store page, your help docs, your game. Somebody wrote it, or a tool produced it, and it reads fine to everyone on your team. Now you want to sell in Taiwan, so you run it through a converter. The output is full of Traditional characters. It looks done. It is not done: conversion gives you Traditional characters, not Taiwan Chinese. A human review service is the step that closes that gap.

This guide explains where conversion goes wrong, what your three options are once you have a Simplified version, what a review pass actually does to fix it, and what it costs. If you are starting from English instead, the game localization guide covers that path; this page is for the case where the Simplified version already exists.

▍Where conversion goes wrong — three layers

Conversion fails in three different places, and only the first one is visible. A spell-checker will pass a converted text. A Taiwan reader will not.

▍1. One-to-many characters — the converter has to guess

Some Simplified characters map to two or more Traditional characters, and only context decides which one. A converter guesses from the surrounding words; when it guesses wrong, you get a wrong character, not just a wrong word. These are the mappings a reviewer checks first:

SimplifiedCorrect Traditional (Taiwan)Depends on meaning
发發 / 髮to send out, to emit — vs. hair
干乾 / 干 / 幹dry — vs. to do, to work
面麵 / 面noodles — vs. face, surface
云雲 / 云cloud — vs. a surname
历歷 / 曆experience — vs. calendar
只隻 / 只classifier for animals — vs. only

A wrong pick here is not a style issue. 頭髮 (hair) rendered as 發頭, or 乾淨 (clean) rendered with the wrong 乾, is a visible error on a live page — and it is exactly the kind of error a converter produces silently, because it had to choose and it chose wrong.

▍2. Vocabulary — the words conversion cannot see

The second layer is invisible to conversion entirely. Words like 軟件, 視頻, and 網絡 are already valid Traditional characters. A converter looks at them, finds nothing to change, and leaves them exactly as they are — and every one of them reads as Mainland Chinese to a Taiwan reader. These are the pairs a human reviewer corrects most often:

This is the layer that decides whether Taiwan readers trust the page. A site that writes 視頻 and 質量 throughout is not a site with a few typos — it is a site that was clearly written for somewhere else. More pairs are collected on the site's terminology glossary, and the free Taiwan wording checker will flag the common ones in a pasted paragraph in seconds. The checker is a good first filter; it is not a substitute for a full review, because it only sees the words on its list.

▍3. Tone and locale conventions — conversion touches neither

The third layer is everything around the words. Dates are written 2026/11/26 or 2026年11月26日 in Taiwan; prices are in NT$; Chinese text uses full-width punctuation (，。；：). Greetings follow Taiwan habit: 雙旦 is a Mainland e-commerce coinage for the Christmas–New Year shopping season, and 春節快樂 is the Mainland greeting — a Taiwan page says 新年快樂 and, for the occasion, 恭喜發財. Conversion changes characters; it does not change habits. A reviewer who lives in Taiwan does not need to look these up — they are the words they hear every year.

Even the tool vendors say so: Taia, a translation platform, writes in its own Chinese translation documentation that converted text "often reads as written for another market" and recommends having "a native linguist review the converted version." That is the honest position: conversion is a starting point, and the review is the step that makes it a Taiwan version.

▍What the review pass actually does — five steps

A review of a converted text is not a read-through. It runs five steps in order.

1. Character mapping audit. Every one-to-many character is checked against its context — 發 vs. 髮, 乾 vs. 干 vs. 幹 — because a wrong pick here is a wrong word on a live page.

2. Wording pass. The full text is corrected to Taiwan vocabulary, against a glossary — yours, or the site's terminology glossary — so the same term gets the same Taiwan translation on every page.

3. Accuracy against the source. The reviewer reads the Chinese against your English or Simplified source, line by line: mistranslations, omissions, additions, numbers that changed along the way. A sentence can be perfectly grammatical and still say the wrong thing.

4. Tone and locale conventions. Form of address (您 vs. 你), formality, dates, currency, punctuation, and seasonal language are checked against Taiwan practice.

5. Consistency and UI fit. Product names, feature names, and button labels are checked for consistency across the whole set — and, where the text is UI, that the strings fit the space they have to live in. 登入, not 登錄; 設定, not 設置; 預設, not 默認.

My judgment, not a measurement: review lowers risk; it does not eliminate it. No reviewer can guarantee zero errors in a large site, and anyone who promises that is selling certainty they do not have. What review does guarantee is that a second, qualified, native pair of eyes has read every line against the source — which is the strongest practical check a localization can get.
