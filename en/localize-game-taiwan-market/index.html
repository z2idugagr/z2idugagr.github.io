How to Localize Your Game for the Taiwan Market: Store Page, In-Game Text, and Taiwan Wording

Hank Chen ・ November 20, 2026 ・ About 7 minutes

Taiwan is its own localization — not a free extra that comes with Simplified Chinese. Steam treats Traditional Chinese (zh-TW) and Simplified Chinese (zh-CN) as two separate languages, with separate store pages, separate language codes (tchinese and schinese), and separate audiences. A Taiwan player who opens your store page and finds only Simplified Chinese reads it the same way you would read a page written for a different country: the words are understandable, but the page clearly was not made for them.

This guide is the practical version: four steps, what each one costs, how to set it up in Steamworks, and a pre-launch checklist. No market-size claims, no conversion-rate promises — just the work, in order.

▍Step 1: Localize the store page first

The store page is everything a player sees before buying — and it is the cheapest part of the job. Title, short description, the "About This Game" section, and tags: that is what decides whether a Taiwan player wishlists your game or scrolls past it. In-game text matters after the purchase; the store page matters before it.

Do the store page first for a second reason: it is a test. A localized store page lets you watch the wishlist response from Taiwan before you commit to translating the whole game. Most publishers I work with start here, check the response, and then decide about in-game text. That sequencing is the point of a store-page-first approach.

What belongs in a store page localization: the game title (localized name, not a transliteration), the short description, the full "About This Game" text, feature lists, and tag suggestions. Capsule images are graphics, not text — if your capsule has English words baked into the image, that is a separate art task, not a translation task.

▍Step 2: Localize the in-game text

Once the store page is done, the in-game text is menus, UI strings, dialogue, and subtitles. This is where most "we already have Chinese" projects go wrong: the game ships with Simplified Chinese text, and nobody checks what Taiwan players actually see.

UI strings have a length problem that store pages do not. A button that fits "Settings" in English has to fit 設定 in Traditional Chinese — usually fine — but a converted string that is longer, or a menu label translated word-for-word from English, will overflow or wrap. Plan for a UI pass after translation: check every button, tooltip, and menu label in-game, not in a spreadsheet.

Subtitles and dialogue need the same treatment as any other Taiwan text. Spoken lines should read like people talk in Taiwan, not like a textbook. If your game has voice acting in English with Traditional Chinese subtitles, the subtitles carry the whole tone — get them right and the game feels local; get them wrong and every cutscene reminds the player the game was not made for them.

▍Step 3: Fix the Taiwan wording — the conversion trap

Converting Simplified Chinese to Traditional characters is not localization. A conversion tool changes the glyphs, not the wording. The output is perfectly valid Traditional Chinese — and instantly reads as Mainland wording to anyone in Taiwan. These are the giveaways I see most often on game pages and in game text:

The rule is the same as for websites: keep what both sides use, replace what only the Mainland uses. More pairs are collected on the site's terminology glossary. ▍Step 4: Set it up in Steamworks — and verify it

Steam offers full store page, client, and API localization for 30 languages, and Traditional Chinese is one of them. In Steamworks, add Traditional Chinese as a store language (language code tchinese) and enter the localized store page text there — it is a separate language slot from Simplified Chinese (schinese), not a variant of it.

Then set the game's supported languages separately. Store page language and in-game language support are two different settings: a game can list Traditional Chinese as an interface or subtitle language even if its store page is not translated yet, and the reverse is also possible. Set both, and set them honestly — list only what the game actually supports. Players check the language table on the store page before buying, and a wrong entry is a refund waiting to happen.

Verify before launch, in a Taiwan language setting. Open the store page with Steam's language set to Traditional Chinese and confirm the localized title, description, and tags actually show — not the English fallback. Then check the in-game language list on the same page. If the localized name does not appear, the language was added to the wrong slot or the page has not been published for that language yet.

Steam's own client interface is available in Traditional Chinese, so Taiwan players browse, buy, and play in zh-TW by default. When your page is English-only, it stands out in their feed for the wrong reason.

▍When to do it: the calendar that decides your deadline

Steam Winter Sale — December 17 to January 4. The biggest sale window of the year for store page traffic, and the one most developers plan their localization around. A Taiwan store page should be live well before December 17, not during the sale. My Chinese-language Winter Sale guide covers the same window in detail: Steam 冬季特賣商店頁進台灣市場.

Steam Awards nomination — November 23 to 30. Nomination season puts store pages in front of players who are browsing and voting. If your game is in the conversation, a Taiwan store page should already be up when the voting starts.

Steam Next Fest (February 2027) — registration closes January 10, 2027. If you are entering the February Next Fest, your demo's store page is the page players land on. Get the Taiwan version done before the registration deadline, not after — the deadline is a natural forcing function, use it.
