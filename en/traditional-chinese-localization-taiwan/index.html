Traditional Chinese Localization for Taiwan: What "Taiwan" Actually Changes

Hank Chen ・ November 27, 2026 ・ About 6 minutes

"Traditional Chinese" is a script, not a market. Three different markets write in it — Taiwan, Hong Kong, and overseas Chinese communities — and they do not write the same Chinese. If you order "Traditional Chinese" without naming the market, you get whichever version your vendor happens to have on hand. For a brand entering Taiwan, that is how a launch ships in the wrong Chinese: every character is correct, and every word tells the reader the content was written for somewhere else.

This guide is the short version of what changes when the market is Taiwan. The three long-form guides behind it go deeper on each layer: Taiwan vs Hong Kong Traditional Chinese covers the Taiwan–Hong Kong differences in full, What a Native Taiwan Reviewer Actually Checks covers the seven checks a reviewer runs, and Simplified to Taiwan Traditional Chinese: A Human Review Service covers what happens when you already have a Simplified version. This page is the decision version: what to confirm before you buy, and what it costs.

▍What "Taiwan" changes — three layers

The differences sit in three places, and only the first one is visible to a spell-checker. A text can pass every automated check and still read as foreign in Taiwan.

▍1. Wording — the same script, different words

This is the layer that decides whether Taiwan readers trust the page. Words like 軟件, 視頻, and 網絡 are already valid Traditional characters. A converter finds nothing to change and leaves them exactly as they are — and every one of them reads as Mainland Chinese to a Taiwan reader. These are the pairs a Taiwan version corrects most often:

What a generic version saysWhat Taiwan readers expectMeaning
軟件軟體software
視頻影片video
網絡網路network / internet
信息資訊information
質量品質quality
鼠標滑鼠mouse

A site that writes 視頻 and 質量 throughout is not a site with a few typos — it is a site that was clearly written for somewhere else. More pairs are collected on the site's terminology glossary, and the free Taiwan wording checker will flag the common ones in a pasted paragraph in seconds. The checker is a good first filter; it is not a substitute for a full localization, because it only sees the words on its list.

Even the big vendors say so: Stepes writes on its own Chinese translation page that "a Taiwan version should be localized by qualified Taiwan Chinese linguists rather than created through simple character conversion." Asialocalize's Taiwan business guide puts it the same way: "Companies should localize for Taiwanese vocabulary, tone and user expectations — not simply convert Simplified Chinese characters." When your competitors' own documentation agrees with your positioning, the positioning is not a sales claim. It is the state of the field.

▍2. Character forms — Taiwan and Hong Kong do not write identically

The second layer is the characters themselves. Taiwan and Hong Kong both write Traditional Chinese, but they do not always write the same Traditional character: 裡 is the Taiwan form, 裏 the Hong Kong form. Written Cantonese — 佢, 冇, 曱甴 — appears in Hong Kong copy and never in Taiwan copy. A Hong Kong version shipped to Taiwan readers is not wrong in the way a Simplified version is wrong, but it is visibly not theirs. The full comparison, including the three-way table for words that are wrong in both markets, is in Taiwan vs Hong Kong Traditional Chinese.

The same logic applies in reverse to a Simplified source: some Simplified characters map to two or more Traditional characters, and only context decides which one — 發 becomes 發 or 髮, 乾 becomes 乾, 干, or 幹. A converter guesses; a reviewer checks. That layer is covered in full in the human review guide.

▍3. Conventions — dates, money, channels, greetings

The third layer is everything around the words. Dates are written 2026/11/26 or 2026年11月26日 in Taiwan; prices are in NT$; Chinese text uses full-width punctuation (，。；：); the contact channel Taiwan readers actually use is LINE, not email alone. Greetings follow Taiwan habit: 雙旦 is a Mainland e-commerce coinage for the Christmas–New Year shopping season, and 春節快樂 is the Mainland greeting — a Taiwan page says 新年快樂 and, for the occasion, 恭喜發財. None of this is in any character table. A localizer who lives in Taiwan does not need to look it up — it is the language they hear every year.

▍The platform defaults are already on Taiwan's side

If your content ships on a platform, the platform has usually already picked Taiwan for you. Steam supports 30 languages and has exactly one Traditional Chinese: zh-TW, listed as tchinese. There is no separate Hong Kong option to buy or build — for a Steam store page, the Taiwan version is the Traditional Chinese version. Microsoft's language documentation lists zh-HK as "No longer used. See zh-TW." The Unicode CLDR default for zh-Hant is zh-Hant-TW. Three independent defaults, one answer: when a system has to pick one Traditional Chinese, it picks Taiwan.

Keywords Studios, a large game localization vendor, makes the same point from the vendor side: "Traditional Chinese is not one single audience," and a Taiwan version (zh-TW) and a Hong Kong version (zh-HK) differ in word choice and colloquial phrases — their example is 草莓 in Taiwan against 士多啤梨 in Hong Kong. The practical consequence: if you are localizing a game, the store page and the in-game text should be briefed as zh-TW from the start, not as generic "Traditional Chinese" to be fixed later. Most buyers need one version. The question is which one — and when a second one earns its cost.

The rule behind the table: only buy a second version when the text is long enough, and the voice matters enough, for the markets to diverge. For everything else, one Taiwan version is the default — and it is the default the platforms already chose. ▍Before you brief a vendor — the checklist

CheckPass standard
Locale stated explicitlyzh-TW — not a generic zh-Hant that happens to fall back to Taiwan
Audience namedTaiwan readers, Hong Kong readers, or both — written in the brief, not assumed
Source text includedThe original English (or Simplified) source, so the localizer can compare against it
Context providedScreenshots or links showing where each string appears
Glossary providedProduct names, feature names, and do-not-translate terms listed
One contactOne person on your side to answer questions during the job

Asialocalize's guide states the first item as a rule, not a suggestion: "A complete brief should state Traditional Chinese for Taiwan (zh-TW) and define the intended audience and content type." If your brief does not name the locale, you have not briefed a Taiwan localization — you have briefed a translation, and the version you get back will be whichever one the vendor writes by default.
