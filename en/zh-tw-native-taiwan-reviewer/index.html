What a Native Taiwan Reviewer Actually Checks in Your Traditional Chinese

Hank Chen ・ November 25, 2026 ・ About 6 minutes

"Traditional Chinese" on your website is a claim. Who reviewed it decides whether Taiwan readers believe it. A conversion tool can change Simplified characters into Traditional ones in seconds. A translator can render your English into fluent Chinese in days. Neither of those steps answers the question your Taiwan customers actually ask when they land on your page: was this written for us?

That question is answered by the reviewer — the last person to touch the text before it goes live, and the only one whose job is to read it the way a Taiwan reader will. This guide explains what a native Taiwan reviewer actually checks, how that differs from proofreading and from machine checks, and when review is the right job versus a full re-localization.

▍What "native Taiwan reviewer" means — and what it doesn't

Three distinctions decide whether a reviewer can do this job at all.

Native to Taiwan, not just a Chinese speaker. A reviewer who lives and works in Taiwan reads and writes Taiwan Mandarin every day — the vocabulary, the register, the platform conventions. A fluent speaker from another Chinese-speaking region can be an excellent translator and still miss Taiwan wording, because the differences are habits, not knowledge: nobody looks up whether Taiwan writes 軟體 or 軟件; they just write the one they grew up with.

A reviewer, not the translator. Professional review is done by a second qualified linguist, not the person who produced the translation. The translator knows what they meant to write; the reviewer sees what is actually on the page. A bilingual reviewer also compares the text against the source — that is what separates review from proofreading, which reads the target text alone and cannot catch a mistranslation that happens to be grammatical.

A reviewer, not a machine. Automated checks catch spelling errors and missing placeholders. They cannot judge whether a sentence sounds like something a Taiwan person would say, whether the tone fits the audience, or whether a reference lands. Linguistic quality assurance is a human judgment against agreed criteria — accuracy, terminology, tone, locale conventions, and cultural fit.

▍The seven checks

A review pass runs the same seven checks on every page. The first three catch most of the problems; the last four are what separate a careful review from a quick read-through.

▍1. Wording — the giveaway pairs

Most of what a Taiwan reader notices first is vocabulary. The characters are all Traditional; the words are simply not the words Taiwan uses. These are the pairs a native Taiwan reviewer corrects most often:

Every one of these words is correct Traditional Chinese. That is exactly the problem: a conversion tool considers them all fine, and a non-native reviewer may too. More pairs are collected on the site's terminology glossary.

▍2. Accuracy against the source

The reviewer reads the Chinese against your English — or Simplified Chinese — source, line by line. Mistranslations, omissions, additions, and numbers that changed along the way. A sentence can be perfectly grammatical and still say the wrong thing. That is the most expensive kind of error, because no spell-checker will ever flag it — and because it is the error your customers find first.

▍3. Tone and register

Who is being addressed, and how formally? Marketing copy, help docs, and legal pages do not speak the same way in Taiwan any more than they do in English. The reviewer checks the form of address (您 vs. 你), the level of formality, and whether the voice stays consistent from the homepage to the checkout page. A page that switches register mid-paragraph reads as if two different companies wrote it.

▍4. Locale conventions

Dates, currency, numbers, and punctuation follow Taiwan practice. Dates are written 2026/11/25 or 2026年11月25日; prices are in NT$; Chinese text uses full-width punctuation (，。；：). Official forms in Taiwan use the Republic of China calendar year (民國); marketing copy uses the Gregorian year. Getting this wrong does not confuse anyone — it just signals that the page was not made for Taiwan.

▍5. UI strings and length

Buttons, menus, and labels get their own check, because they break differently from paragraphs. The reviewer checks that the words are the ones Taiwan apps actually use — 登入, not 登錄; 設定, not 設置; 預設, not 默認 — and that the strings fit the space they have to live in. A correct translation that overflows its button is still a defect, and it is one only a reviewer who sees the string in context can catch.

▍6. Consistency

The same term gets the same translation on every page. The reviewer works from a glossary — yours, or the site's terminology glossary — and flags every place the same product name, feature, or button label was translated two different ways. Inconsistency is the defect customers notice second, right after wrong wording, and it is the one that grows with every new page you add.

▍7. Cultural fit

Idioms, jokes, references, and seasonal language are checked against what Taiwan readers actually say. 雙旦 is a Mainland e-commerce coinage for the Christmas–New Year shopping season; a Taiwan page should say 聖誕加跨年. 春節快樂 is the Mainland greeting; in Taiwan the safe greetings are 新年快樂 and, for the occasion, 恭喜發財. A reviewer who lives in Taiwan does not need to look these up — they are the words they hear every year.

▍What review is not

Not a rewrite. If most sentences need to be rewritten rather than corrected, the right job is a re-localization, not a review. A good reviewer tells you that instead of charging you for a review that papers over a bad translation.

Not a legal certification. Review checks language quality, not legal compliance. Certified or notarized translation is a different service, and I do not offer it.

Not slogan transcreation. A tagline that needs to be reinvented for Taiwan is creative work, not review. It is quoted separately.

My judgment, not a measurement: review lowers risk; it does not eliminate it. No reviewer can guarantee zero errors in a large site, and anyone who promises that is selling certainty they do not have. What review does guarantee is that a second, qualified, native pair of eyes has read every line against the source — which is the strongest practical check a localization can get.
