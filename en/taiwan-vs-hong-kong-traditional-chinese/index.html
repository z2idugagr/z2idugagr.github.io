Taiwan vs Hong Kong Traditional Chinese: Same Script, Different Words

Hank Chen ・ November 24, 2026 ・ About 6 minutes

"Traditional Chinese" names a script, not a market. Taiwan and Hong Kong both write in Traditional characters — but they standardize those characters differently, use different everyday words, and, in Hong Kong's case, write in a register shaped by Cantonese. If you ship one "Traditional Chinese" version of your product, you are choosing a side whether you mean to or not. This guide shows where the two versions actually differ, what the platforms already decided for you, and how to choose when your budget only covers one.

The differences sit in three layers: vocabulary, character forms, and written Cantonese. None of them is fixed by converting Simplified Chinese to Traditional characters — a conversion changes the glyphs, not the words, and it produces text that reads wrong in both markets.

▍The first difference: vocabulary — the words that give you away

Most of what a Taiwan reader notices first is vocabulary. The characters are all Traditional; the words are simply not the words Taiwan uses. These are the pairs I correct most often:

Not every word differs — both sides write 影片 for a video clip, for example. The trap is assuming all of them are shared. A page that says 軟件 and 網絡 in Taiwan reads the way a page that says "lift" and "flat" reads in the United States: understandable, and unmistakably written for somewhere else. More pairs are collected on the site's terminology glossary.

▍The second difference: character forms — same character, different standard

Taiwan and Hong Kong do not always agree on which shape of a character is standard. Taiwan follows the Ministry of Education's Standard Form of National Characters (國字標準字體), taught in schools and used in textbooks. Hong Kong follows its own glyph guidance, and in Hong Kong print both forms of some characters have long circulated. The classic example is 裡 vs. 裏 — the same character, "inside": Taiwan standardizes 裡, while 裏 has a strong foothold in Hong Kong publishing.

A Taiwan reader sees 裏 as a variant form, not a mistake. But a page full of Hong Kong-standard forms signals "not made for Taiwan" just as clearly as the wrong vocabulary does. Character forms are the quietest layer of the difference — and the one a conversion tool can never fix, because the tool considers both forms correct.

▍The third difference: written Cantonese — characters that only exist in Hong Kong

Hong Kong people speak Cantonese and write in Standard Chinese — but informal Hong Kong writing borrows Cantonese words, and some of them have characters of their own. 佢 means "he" or "she"; 冇 means "not have"; 曱甴 means "cockroach". None of these characters exists in Taiwan's written vocabulary, and a Taiwan reader meeting them in marketing copy or a UI string can be genuinely stuck — not confused about tone, but unable to read the word at all.

Formal Hong Kong writing mostly avoids written Cantonese. But marketing copy, social posts, and UI strings are exactly where it slips in, because that is where writers write the way people talk. If your Hong Kong version was written by a Hong Kong copywriter, check it for written Cantonese before you assume it doubles as a Taiwan version. It usually does not.

▍The trap in the middle: converted Simplified Chinese fails in both markets

Before you compare Taiwan and Hong Kong, check you are not shipping a third thing by accident: Simplified Chinese with Traditional glyphs. A conversion tool changes the characters and stops. The output is perfectly valid Traditional Chinese — and instantly reads as Mainland wording to readers in both Taiwan and Hong Kong:

What conversion producesTaiwanHong Kong
視頻影片影片
信息資訊資訊
鼠標滑鼠滑鼠
內存記憶體記憶體
軟件軟體軟件

Notice the pattern: on several of these words Taiwan and Hong Kong agree — both write 影片, 資訊, 滑鼠, 記憶體 — and the converted word is wrong in both places. Only the last row splits them. That is why "convert, then check" is not a workflow: the check has to be done by someone who knows which market the page is for. My Chinese-language guide covers the same trap in detail: 簡轉繁不是在地化.

▍What the platforms do — Taiwan is the default

You do not have to take my word for which version is the default. The platforms already decided.

Steam offers exactly one Traditional Chinese option — zh-TW, Steamworks code tchinese — among its 30 fully supported languages. There is no separate Hong Kong Chinese option for a store page or a game. The Traditional Chinese your players see on Steam is Taiwan wording by default, and a game that lists Traditional Chinese is promising Taiwan players a page written for them.

Microsoft retired the Hong Kong tag. The Windows language-pack documentation lists "Chinese (Traditional, Hong Kong SAR)" (zh-HK) with the note "No longer used. See zh-TW." The platform's own guidance points Hong Kong users at the Taiwan pack.

Unicode's CLDR — the locale database most software resolves language through — does the same thing. A bare zh-Hant tag resolves to zh-Hant-TW; Hong Kong is the override you have to ask for, not the default you get.

My judgment, not a measurement: if you can only ship one Traditional Chinese version, ship Taiwan wording. Hong Kong readers read standard written Chinese every day, so a Taiwan-worded page reads as standard to them; a Hong Kong-worded page with written Cantonese reads as a different register in Taiwan. That asymmetry is why Taiwan is the safe single version — not because Hong Kong wording is wrong, but because Taiwan wording travels further.

▍One version or two?

Taiwan is the market: one Taiwan version, done. Do not pay for a second version you do not need. One well-localized zh-TW version covers your Steam store page, your website, and your in-game text for Taiwan — and, by the platform defaults above, it is also the version Hong Kong users will be shown if you ship nothing else.

Hong Kong is also a real market: add a Hong Kong review pass, not a second translation. Most sentences stay unchanged; only the vocabulary layer changes — 軟體 to 軟件, 網路 to 網絡, 計程車 to 的士 — plus a sweep for written Cantonese. A review pass is priced as a review, US$0.04 per Chinese character, which is a fraction of a second full localization. See the review service for how that works.

A full second localization only makes sense when Hong Kong is a primary market and the copy is marketing-heavy. If your Hong Kong landing page, ads, and social copy are doing real work, they deserve a Hong Kong copywriter's pass. If the Hong Kong audience is incidental, the review pass is enough — and the money is better spent on the Taiwan version being excellent.
